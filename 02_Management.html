Unit 1: Meaning, nature and evolution

Core notes / Samajh

Management coordinates people and resources to achieve organisational objectives efficiently (minimum waste) and effectively (goal attainment). Functions commonly include planning, organising, staffing, directing and controlling. It is goal-oriented, continuous, pervasive, multidimensional and performed through people. Management is a discipline/systematic body of knowledge, a practice requiring skill, and partly an art; it is not an exact science because human situations vary. Levels: top (strategy), middle (coordination), supervisory (day-to-day execution). Functional areas include marketing, finance, operations, HR and administration.

Key concepts & application

F.W. Taylor’s scientific management: science not rule of thumb, harmony not discord, cooperation, maximum output and development of each worker. Techniques included time study, standardisation, differential piece-rate and functional foremanship. Henri Fayol’s administrative principles include division of work, authority-responsibility, discipline, unity of command, unity of direction, scalar chain, equity, initiative and esprit de corps.

Exam-ready tip: For compare/contrast: Taylor focused mainly on shop-floor efficiency; Fayol on organisation-wide administration. Apply principles flexibly rather than mechanically.

Detailed explanation / In depth

Management is needed in a small shop, a college society, a government department and a multinational—although scale and structure differ. Efficiency asks whether resources were used economically; effectiveness asks whether the right objective was achieved. An organisation can be efficient but ineffective (producing the wrong product cheaply) or effective but inefficient (meeting a goal with excessive waste). Managers balance both while responding to stakeholders and uncertainty. Their time allocation varies by level: top managers focus more on long-term direction; supervisors spend more time on immediate operations and people support.

Worked application / Answer mein kaise likhein

Fayol’s principles are guidelines, not rigid laws. Unity of command means one employee should ordinarily receive orders from one supervisor; functional expertise may complicate this in matrix structures. Scalar chain clarifies authority paths, while a “gang plank” permits urgent lateral communication with proper awareness. Taylor’s time and motion studies sought a better method for work, but modern application must also consider worker dignity, safety, learning and quality—not output speed alone.

Unit 2: Planning and decision-making

Core notes / Samajh

Planning decides in advance what to do, how, when and by whom. Purposes: direction, reduced uncertainty, coordination, resource use and control standards. Steps: set objectives → analyse premises/environment → identify alternatives → evaluate → select → supporting plans/budgets → implement → review. Plans include objectives, strategies, policies, procedures, rules, programmes and budgets. MBO aligns measurable employee objectives with organisational goals through participation, periodic review and feedback; drawbacks include excessive paperwork, short-termism and inflexible targets.

Key concepts & application

Decision process: define problem → gather relevant information → generate alternatives → evaluate consequences/risks → choose → implement → monitor. Programmed decisions are routine; non-programmed are novel. Decisions may be strategic/tactical/operational, individual/group, certain/risky/uncertain. Example: selecting a supplier using cost, quality, delivery and reliability criteria instead of price alone.

Exam-ready tip: Planning is future-oriented but not a guarantee; assumptions can change. Give a real organisational example and explain why evaluation and follow-up matter.

Detailed explanation / In depth

Objectives should be specific enough to guide action and measurable where possible. A strategy sets broad direction; a policy guides recurring judgment; a procedure lists steps; a rule prescribes or prohibits a specific action; a budget quantifies expected resources/results. These are related but not interchangeable. Planning premises are assumptions about future conditions such as demand, regulation or input price. Scenario planning can prepare alternatives when a single forecast is unreliable.

Worked application / Answer mein kaise likhein

MBO works best when goals are jointly understood, aligned across levels and reviewed through coaching rather than used only as a punishment device. If teams are judged on incompatible targets, local optimisation may harm the organisation—for example, purchasing may minimise unit cost by buying excess stock while finance faces cash pressure. Decision tools include cost-benefit analysis, decision trees, break-even analysis and weighted scoring; state the assumptions and non-financial criteria too.

Unit 3: Organising and delegation

Core notes / Samajh

Organising identifies work, groups activities, assigns duties, establishes relationships and provides resources. Structure may be line, functional, line-and-staff, divisional or matrix. Authority is the legitimate right to decide/direct; responsibility is duty to perform; accountability is answerability for results. Delegation transfers authority for a task but the manager retains ultimate accountability. Elements: assignment of duties, grant of authority and creation of accountability.

Key concepts & application

Centralisation concentrates decision power at higher levels; decentralisation distributes it. Advantages of decentralisation: faster local response, initiative, development of managers and top-level focus. Risks: duplication, inconsistent decisions and weaker control. Span of control is number of subordinates reporting to one manager; optimal span depends on task complexity, staff capability, manager skill and communication systems.

Exam-ready tip: Do not confuse delegation with abdication. A useful answer explains both the benefit and boundary/controls. Draw a small organisation chart if the question asks for structure.

Detailed explanation / In depth

Structure should fit strategy, size, technology and environment. Functional structure builds expertise but may create departmental silos. Divisional structure focuses on product, geography or customer but duplicates functions. Matrix structure shares specialist resources and improves cross-functional work, yet employees may face dual reporting and role conflict. Informal organisation also exists through relationships and networks; it can accelerate communication but may circulate rumours.

Worked application / Answer mein kaise likhein

Effective delegation specifies expected result, limits, authority, resources, reporting and deadline. A manager should delegate outcomes and suitable discretion, then monitor proportionately rather than micromanage. Decentralisation is a design choice, not automatically superior: local decisions can be faster, while central standards may be necessary for safety, brand consistency or legal compliance. Span of control is wider when work is routine, staff are capable and processes are standardised; narrower when work is complex or coaching-intensive.

Unit 4: Coordination and emerging ideas

Core notes / Samajh

Coordination harmonises individual and departmental efforts toward common goals. It is continuous, pervasive, deliberate and the essence of management. Tools include clear objectives, plans, communication, liaison roles, committees, meetings, budgets, standard procedures and integrated information systems. Cooperation is willingness to assist; coordination is systematic alignment of work—both reinforce each other.

Key concepts & application

TQM (Total Quality Management) makes quality everyone’s responsibility through customer focus, continuous improvement, prevention and employee participation. MBE (Management by Exception) directs attention to significant deviations from standards. Core competency is a distinctive capability that creates customer value and is hard to imitate. CSR is responsibility for social/environmental impacts beyond narrow profit maximisation.

Exam-ready tip: For CSR/TQM questions include a practical example, stakeholders affected and measurable outcomes (defects, delivery, safety, customer satisfaction).

Detailed explanation / In depth

Departments have different specialist goals, so coordination connects plans, timing, information and accountability. For example, marketing’s promotion schedule must align with operations’ capacity and finance’s cash plan. Coordination is built into planning and structure, not added only after conflict. Cooperation is a positive willingness to work together; coordination translates shared effort into aligned roles and sequence.

Worked application / Answer mein kaise likhein

TQM is a system-wide philosophy: understand customer requirements, prevent defects, improve processes continuously and involve employees. A quality circle can identify process issues, but staff need authority and management follow-through. MBE concentrates managerial attention on material deviations, so standards and reporting thresholds must be sensible. Core competencies should provide customer value, be transferable across products and be difficult for competitors to imitate. CSR can be assessed through impacts, stakeholder dialogue, legal compliance and transparent reporting—not only donations.

Unit 5: Direction and control

Core notes / Samajh

Direction guides and motivates people to act: supervision, leadership, communication and motivation. Principles include clear instructions, unity of command, appropriate leadership, feedback, direct contact and situational flexibility. Control ensures actual performance aligns with plans: establish standards → measure performance → compare → analyse deviations → corrective action → review. Feedforward, concurrent and feedback controls operate before, during and after work.

Key concepts & application

Budgets express plans quantitatively. Budgetary control compares actual results with budget and investigates variances. Benefits: coordination, cost awareness and accountability. Limitations: estimates can be wrong, rigid budgets discourage initiative, and managers may game targets. Control should be timely, economical, understandable and focused on critical points.

Exam-ready tip: A strong answer links planning and control: plans set standards; control supplies feedback for revised plans. Distinguish corrective action from merely identifying a variance.

Detailed explanation / In depth

Direction brings plans to life through communication, supervision, leadership and motivation. Clear instructions explain what, why, when and quality standards; feedback lets employees surface obstacles. Control is a cycle: establish standards, measure, compare, diagnose variance, take corrective action and learn. Standards may be quantitative (cost, output, error rate) or qualitative (service quality, safety behaviour). Too many metrics create overload and can encourage gaming.

Worked application / Answer mein kaise likhein

A budget is a plan and a control benchmark, not a guarantee. Flexible budgets adjust expected costs for actual activity levels, making comparison fairer when volume changes. Variance analysis asks why actual results differ; it should distinguish controllable from uncontrollable factors. Example: adverse material cost variance might reflect supplier price, waste, product mix or purchase timing. Corrective action could involve process changes or renegotiation, not simply blaming staff. Control should protect learning, ethics and long-term capability.
